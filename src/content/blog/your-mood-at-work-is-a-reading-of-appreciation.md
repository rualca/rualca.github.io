I keep forgetting this and then relearning it: what matters to us at work is whether the people who count appreciate what we do.

I have watched it in myself, in my partner, in friends, in colleagues, and in people I only observed from a distance over the years. The pattern repeats. Across one or two days, sometimes one or two weeks, we swing from standing at the bow of the ship with our arms wide to feeling that none of it matters. Nothing about the work changed. What changed is how our boss, our management and our peers see the work we are doing.

The upswing looks like this. You propose something and management is immediately on board: this is great, we are re-tasking around it, this is valuable to the company. That week you have the best job you have ever had.

The downswing looks like this. You propose something and nobody answers. Then you find out about a meeting you were not invited to. Then you hear your peers praised for work adjacent to yours. Intellectually you know it should not touch you — you did not like the company anyway, the management is what it is, half the team is building its own little empire. None of that reasoning reaches the part of you that stops wanting to get up in the morning, that stops wanting Sunday because Monday follows it. The feeling is dread, and a quiet sense of being worthless.

I think this deserves far more attention than it gets, because the mechanism underneath is not vanity. Work is how most of us produce something the world can see, and the signal that it landed is appreciation from the people who decide what counts: your manager, your peers, and then the wider organisation. That is the currency. When it stops arriving, something primary goes quiet.

So the first question, when you feel low, is not "what is wrong with me". It is: where am I at work right now? How respected is my work, today, by the people who decide?

The answer usually explains more than any other single question I know. And it turns over fast. You can have eighteen months of being respected and listened to, of being basically fine with Monday. Then a management change, or one person added to the team, and the register shifts. Six weeks later you are furniture, and probably furniture they are looking to replace.

Which leads to the second question, and this one has to be answered honestly: can I change this from where I stand, or do I need to be somewhere else?

"Can I change it" is not optimism. It has a test. Name the specific thing you would need — a different manager, a decision reversed, a scope you are allowed to own, someone removed from the loop — and ask whether that thing is available this quarter. If it is not, waiting is not a strategy. Waiting is the expensive option, because the version of you that stopped wanting Monday does not recover by being patient.

The amount of appreciation a traditional corporate job can supply is shrinking, and I expect it to keep shrinking. Not because the people are worse than they were, but because the organisations are less sure of themselves than they used to be, and an organisation that is not sure of itself cannot keep telling anyone that their work matters.

My conclusion is narrower than "go and build your own thing". It is this: attach yourself to a problem that has a customer, a cost or a compliance deadline attached, and make sure the people who decide can see that. Problems with an outside party are the ones that survive a reorganisation.

And if you are low right now, run the list. Are you respected at work? Do people listen to your ideas? Does management value you? Do your peers? If the answer is no, and you cannot see a fast path to changing it, start planning the exit. It may take one or two other jobs before you land somewhere that gives you what this one does not, and that is fine.

Pay attention to the line between how the work is going and how you are doing. It is one of the more honest correlations in a working life, and nobody is going to measure it for you.
