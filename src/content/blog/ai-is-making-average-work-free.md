Talking to a friend in the industry recently, there was one thing they said I could not shake: AI is making average work free.

Every team I know can now produce a decent first draft of almost anything. A service skeleton, a migration plan, a design document, the deck for the board. What used to take an afternoon takes twenty minutes and a review.

That sounds like good news, and for a while it is. The floor dropped for everyone at the same time, which means nobody gained an advantage from it. What happens next is the part worth thinking about.

The scarce thing is no longer execution. It is having a point of view worth executing.

A point of view is not a personality, and it is not confidence. It is the set of answers you would still defend when the room pushes back: what you would build first with a year and three engineers, which tradeoff you refuse to make even when the deadline moves, what you think the team is getting wrong and why. Most people cannot produce those answers on request, and the reason is not talent.

We were trained out of it slowly. School rewarded the correct answer, and there was always one. Early jobs rewarded not being difficult, in rooms where being difficult had a price. By the time someone is senior enough for their opinion to carry, they have spent fifteen years practising the opposite, and the muscle has quietly atrophied.

Hiring shows it more clearly than anything else I do.

The senior engineers I remember from interviews are rarely the ones who solved the exercise fastest. They are the ones who told me, somewhere in the conversation, that part of my premise was wrong. Not as a trick and not rudely. They simply could not help it: they had already thought about the problem before I finished describing it, and a view had formed.

That quality is about to become most of the job.

There is a version of the next few years where everyone has the same tools and the same ability to produce a competent implementation of whatever the ticket says. In that version, the difference between two engineers is not how fast they write. It is which tickets get written, and which ones should never have existed.

The uncomfortable part is that this does not only apply to people who write code. It applies to me. A CTO without an opinion of their own is a very expensive routing layer between the CEO and the team. I can produce a strategy document in ten minutes now. What I cannot produce on demand is the conviction about which strategy is wrong, and that is the part anyone hired me for.

So the answer is not better prompting.

The answer is getting your own thinking back, and it is less glamorous than it sounds. Reading things nobody in your feed is reading. Noticing what actually bothers you about how your industry works, and writing it down instead of letting it pass. Disagreeing out loud once, in a place where it costs you something small.

There is a cheap test. Take the last three things you thought were wrong at work, and check whether anyone else knows you thought them. If the answer is no, you have been running on borrowed ideas and calling it alignment.

AI did not create this problem. It removed the last excuse for it.

A team that executes flawlessly on an average idea is now worth exactly what the idea was worth, which is less than it cost to build.
