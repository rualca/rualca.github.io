Daniel Miessler made an argument recently that I think is right about the diagnosis and incomplete about the prescription: [focus on problems instead of companies](https://danielmiessler.com/blog/focus-on-problems-not-companies). Companies are panicking, he says, so stop treating one as a refuge and start building your work around the problems your experience lets you solve.

The diagnosis, from inside a company, is hard to argue with. I have spent twelve years scaling engineering organisations, six engineers to around forty in one of them, and migrations in three more. In every case the pressure came from the same place: a leadership team that could no longer answer the question of what the company looks like in five years, and therefore could not hold a strategy still long enough for the organisation to execute it. That is not malice and it is rarely incompetence. It is the cost of not knowing, and it lands on the people furthest from the decision.

Where I would push back is on the unit. A company is not a container you put stability into. It is a bundle of problems that somebody agreed to keep solving, with a contract, a payroll and a liability attached. When the bundle stops making sense, the container dissolves and the problems do not — which is exactly Miessler's point, arrived at from the other direction.

The trouble is that the prescription assumes a shape most people do not have. A single person can pick a problem and offer a solution next week. A team of forty cannot. The problems I work on now are not the kind one person solves on the side: a computer vision system inspecting fabric on a line that cannot stop, a model that has to survive losing the network at the edge, an audit trail you can hand to a regulator under the EU AI Act. Those need capital, contracts, on-call rotas, and someone who signs. That is what a company is for.

So the frame I actually use is narrower. Inside an organisation, the question is not "which company is safe" — none of them are, and never were. It is **which problem am I attached to, and does anyone outside my own team still care about it?** The problems that survive a reorganisation are the ones with a customer, a cost, or a compliance deadline attached. Everything else is a project that exists because a slide said so.

That is testable, and it does not require quitting anything. Describe the problem you work on in one sentence, without naming your team or your title. If the sentence is about the business, you can survive the next reorganisation. If the sentence only makes sense internally, you are attached to the container, not the content.

The stability that actually holds is not the company and not the side project. It is being the person who can name the problem, say what it costs, and show the work. That travels.
